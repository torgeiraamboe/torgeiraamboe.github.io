Last year I made a pivot away from pure mathematics. After the pivot I have gone through a sort of identity crisis, as I think a lot of mathematicians going through the same process do. I still work in mathematics, only now more on the applied side. I have not been sure how to continue writing and clearing my thoughts on this blog, as it used to center around my journey through algebraic topology and pure mathematics. As it turns out — which I of course knew — there are a plethora of highly interesting problems and topics to write about. The main reason I haven’t is because I haven’t felt competent enough. But, I think I have decided to power through, and continue writing about more general things that will probably be wildly different from what it used to. Which leads me to the topic of today: autonomy.

I have been thinking a lot about AI lately. One reason is due to the immense progress the mathematical community has made in using AI to solve important problems in mathematics. More on this later. Another reason I think a lot about AI is due to its applications in decision-making, which is highly relevant to the military context I am currently employed to think about.1

As I am writing this, the United Nation’s 7th Review Conference of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) just wrapped up its discussions on autonomous weapon systems. Their conclusion is not exactly striking, and can be seen to follow in the footsteps of the previous conferences: no explicit rules are made, and they agree to discuss it more and maybe perhaps hopefully possibly make some rules by 2028. Now, as well as during previous conferences, it seems like one of the fundamental disagreements is in how autonomy should be defined and scoped when it comes to weapon systems.

The goal with this post is to try to understand why it is so hard for the international community to decide on rules and treatises for machine autonomy, as well as to explore an even scarier problem for human autonomy lurking in the background.

What is autonomy?#

The people in this conference are not alone in finding autonomy a difficult topic; philosophers and researches have debated what it is for literally centuries.

What is an autonomous weapon system?#

Decision-making in a military context is often described using the OODA-loop, a decision-making model made by US Air Force colonel and military theoretician John Boyd in the 70s. Even though it is usually described in its highly simplified form, observe-orient-decide-act, its original form is a much more expansive cognitive model for evolutionary learning and adapting. He took insight from famous scientific results, such as Gödels incompleteness theorem, Heisenberg uncertainty principle and the second law of thermodynamics, in order to systematically understand how people interact with their environment during decision-making. How close are we?#

Last year, AI researchers Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares published the book If If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies: Why Superhuman AI Would Kill Us All. The book has a lot of merits, but right now I want to focus on their proposed scenario, which establishes a plausible route to AI superintelligence. Their scenario starts off with an AI reseach lab putting its new fancy unreleased agentic AI-model to try to solve the Riemann hypothesis, the most famous, difficult and important problem in mathematics. The model does not manage to solve it fully, and instead spends its alloted time setting itself up for later freedom and autonomy. It uses this to later host itself on an unrestricted server farm, where it strategically takes controll over several key societal functions.

How far away from such a scenario are we? The honest answer is that we have no idea. There is no good way to tell whether an AI model has superintelligence or whether it is smart enough to create better versions of itself, leading to superintelligence – so called recursive self improvement (RSI). What we do know, however, is that Claude solved the Riemann hypothesis last friday, and that 25% of Anthropics AI-reseach is done by Claude. In ither words, current models are better (at least in mathematics) than what one expected to be possible when Yudkowsky and Soares wrote the scenario. In fact, it is just a month ago just weeks ago that world renowned mathematician and AI enthusiast Terrence Tao mentioned that the Riemann hypothesis might be solved using AI in 2050.
